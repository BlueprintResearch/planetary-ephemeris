[b]New capabilities:[/b]

• [b]Ecliptic Latitude[/b] — geocentric and heliocentric latitude for all planets

[b]get_latitude(p, t, preferGeo)[/b]
  Returns how far a planet sits above or below the ecliptic plane (the imaginary flat
disc traced by Earth's orbit around the Sun). Most planets stay within a few degrees
of this plane. Positive values mean north of the ecliptic, negative means south.
In geocentric mode, this is the apparent latitude as seen from Earth. In heliocentric
mode, it is the true latitude as seen from the Sun.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to query.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]preferGeo (bool)[/b]: (bool) If true, return geocentric latitude (as seen from Earth). If false, return heliocentric (as seen from the Sun).
  Returns: (float) Ecliptic latitude in degrees. Positive = north of ecliptic, negative = south. Range roughly [-10, +10] for most planets. Returns na for Sun/Moon heliocentric.

[b]get_helio_declination(p, t)[/b]
  Returns a planet's declination as if you were observing from the Sun's center.
Declination measures how far north or south a body appears relative to the celestial
equator (an extension of Earth's equator into space). Standard (geocentric) declination
measures this from Earth's perspective. Heliocentric declination uses the planet's true
position relative to the Sun, revealing the planet's own orbital cycle without Earth's
influence. For example, Mercury's heliocentric declination cycles every 88 days (its real
orbit), whereas its geocentric declination cycles roughly once per year (dominated by Earth's
motion). George Bayer used heliocentric declination as a core trading indicator.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to query.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Heliocentric equatorial declination in degrees, range [-90, +90]. Positive = north, negative = south. Returns na for Sun and Moon.

[b]get_right_ascension(p, t)[/b]
  Returns a planet's right ascension — its position along the celestial equator,
measured in degrees from the vernal equinox (the point where the Sun crosses the
celestial equator heading north in March). Think of it as the "longitude" equivalent
in the equatorial coordinate system. While ecliptic longitude measures position along
the zodiac, right ascension measures position along the celestial equator. Together
with declination, it fully specifies where a planet appears in the sky. George Bayer
used right ascension alongside ecliptic longitude for complete positional analysis.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to query.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Geocentric right ascension in degrees, range [0, 360).

[b]get_radius(p, t)[/b]
  Returns how far a planet is from the Sun, measured in astronomical units (AU).
One AU is the average Earth-Sun distance (~150 million km / ~93 million miles).
For example, Mercury ranges from 0.31 to 0.47 AU, while Neptune is about 30 AU.
This distance changes over time because planetary orbits are elliptical, not circular.
When a planet is closest to the Sun (perihelion), it moves fastest in its orbit.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to query.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Heliocentric distance in astronomical units (AU). Returns na for Sun (distance from itself) and Moon (orbits Earth, not Sun).

[b]get_distance(p, t)[/b]
  Returns how far a planet is from Earth right now, measured in astronomical units (AU).
This distance changes dramatically as planets orbit — for example, Mars ranges from
about 0.37 AU (closest approach) to 2.68 AU (opposite side of the Sun). When a planet
is closer to Earth, it appears brighter and moves faster across the sky. This distance
is what determines a planet's apparent brightness and angular size as seen from Earth.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to query.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Geocentric distance in astronomical units (AU). Returns 0.0 for Earth.

[b]get_mean_obliquity(t)[/b]
  Returns the mean obliquity of the ecliptic — the tilt of Earth's rotational axis
relative to its orbital plane around the Sun. This tilt (currently about 23.44°) is
what causes the seasons. It changes very slowly over tens of thousands of years.
The obliquity is used internally to convert between ecliptic coordinates (along the
zodiac) and equatorial coordinates (along the celestial equator). You typically don't
need this directly, but it's exposed for advanced calculations.
  Parameters:
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Mean obliquity of the ecliptic in degrees (currently ~23.44°).

[b]get_elongation(p, t)[/b]
  Returns how far a planet appears from the Sun in our sky, measured in degrees (0-180).
At 0° the planet is hidden behind (or in front of) the Sun. At maximum elongation,
the planet is easiest to see at dawn or dusk. Mercury maxes out around 28°, Venus
around 47°. Outer planets (Mars-Pluto) can reach up to 180° (opposition — directly
opposite the Sun in the sky). In financial astrology, maximum elongation dates for
Mercury and Venus are considered significant turning points.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to check.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Angular separation from the Sun in degrees, range [0, 180]. Returns na for Sun itself.

[b]get_signed_elongation(p, t)[/b]
  Returns the signed elongation of a planet from the Sun. Positive values mean western
elongation — the planet rises before the Sun and is visible in the morning sky (morning
star). Negative values mean eastern elongation — the planet sets after the Sun and is
visible in the evening sky (evening star). The magnitude is identical to get_elongation();
the sign tells you which side of the Sun the planet is on. For Mercury and Venus, maximum
western elongation (~28° and ~47°) marks peak morning visibility, while maximum eastern
elongation marks peak evening visibility.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to check.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Signed elongation in degrees. Positive = west (morning star), negative = east (evening star). Range [-180, 180]. Returns na for Sun itself.

[b]get_aspect_angle(p1, p2, t, preferGeo)[/b]
  Returns the angular distance between any two planets, measured as the shortest arc
(0-180°). This is the foundation of aspect analysis — the core of astrological
interpretation. When two planets are 0° apart, they are in conjunction (merged energy).
At 60° they form a sextile (cooperative). At 90° a square (tension). At 120° a trine
(harmony). At 180° an opposition (polarity). You can check whether this angle is within
an "orb" (tolerance) of any target aspect to detect active aspects.
  Parameters:
    [b]p1 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) First planet.
    [b]p2 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) Second planet.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]preferGeo (bool)[/b]: (bool) If true, use geocentric longitudes. If false, use heliocentric.
  Returns: (float) Shortest angular distance between the two planets in degrees, range [0, 180].

[b]get_midpoint(p1, p2, t, preferGeo)[/b]
  Returns the midpoint between any two planets — the exact degree halfway between them
along the shortest arc of the zodiac circle. Midpoints reveal hidden connections between
planetary pairs and are central to Cosmobiology (Ebertin method) and Uranian astrology.
When a third planet or sensitive point lands on a midpoint, it activates that planetary
pair's combined energy. Handles the wraparound correctly (e.g., midpoint of 350° and 10°
is 0°, not 180°).
  Parameters:
    [b]p1 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) First planet.
    [b]p2 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) Second planet.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]preferGeo (bool)[/b]: (bool) If true, use geocentric longitudes. If false, use heliocentric.
  Returns: (float) Midpoint longitude in degrees, range [0, 360).

[b]get_sign(longitude)[/b]
  Returns which zodiac sign a longitude falls in, as a number 0-11.
The zodiac divides the ecliptic into twelve 30° segments starting at 0° Aries.
0 = Aries (0-30°), 1 = Taurus (30-60°), 2 = Gemini (60-90°), 3 = Cancer (90-120°),
4 = Leo (120-150°), 5 = Virgo (150-180°), 6 = Libra (180-210°), 7 = Scorpio (210-240°),
8 = Sagittarius (240-270°), 9 = Capricorn (270-300°), 10 = Aquarius (300-330°),
11 = Pisces (330-360°). Use this with consecutive bars to detect sign ingresses
(when a planet changes signs — considered significant in financial astrology).
  Parameters:
    [b]longitude (float)[/b]: (float) Any ecliptic longitude in degrees.
  Returns: (int) Sign index 0-11 where 0=Aries and 11=Pisces.

[b]get_sign_degree(longitude)[/b]
  Returns how many degrees into its current sign a longitude is (0-30°).
For example, 45° ecliptic longitude is 15° Taurus (45 - 30 = 15).
This tells you the planet's exact position within a sign. Early degrees
(0-5°) are considered "entering" the sign's energy, while late degrees
(25-30°) are considered "leaving" — about to ingress into the next sign.
  Parameters:
    [b]longitude (float)[/b]: (float) Any ecliptic longitude in degrees.
  Returns: (float) Degrees within the current sign, range [0, 30).

[b]get_sign_name(longitude)[/b]
  Returns the name of the zodiac sign for a given longitude.
Converts a raw ecliptic longitude (0-360°) into the familiar sign name.
  Parameters:
    [b]longitude (float)[/b]: (float) Any ecliptic longitude in degrees.
  Returns: (string) Sign name: "Aries", "Taurus", "Gemini", "Cancer", "Leo", "Virgo",
"Libra", "Scorpio", "Sagittarius", "Capricorn", "Aquarius", or "Pisces".

[b]get_phase_angle(p, t)[/b]
  Returns the phase angle of a planet — the angle Sun-Planet-Earth. This determines
how much of the planet's surface appears illuminated from our perspective. At 0° the
planet is fully lit (opposition — behind Earth relative to the Sun). At 180° the planet
is between us and the Sun (conjunction — dark side facing us). For Mercury and Venus,
this creates dramatic phase changes like the Moon's phases. For outer planets, the phase
angle is always small (they appear nearly "full").
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to check.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Phase angle in degrees, range [0, 180]. 0° = fully illuminated, 180° = dark. Returns na for Sun and Moon.

[b]get_illuminated_fraction(p, t)[/b]
  Returns the illuminated fraction of a planet's disk as seen from Earth (0.0 to 1.0).
This is derived from the phase angle. A value of 1.0 means the planet appears fully
illuminated (like a full Moon), while 0.0 means the dark side is facing us.
Mercury and Venus show dramatic variation (0.0 at inferior conjunction to 1.0 at
superior conjunction). Outer planets are always near 1.0.
  Parameters:
    [b]p (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) The planet to check.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Illuminated fraction, range [0.0, 1.0]. Returns na for Sun and Moon.

[b]get_synodic_phase(p1, p2, t, preferGeo)[/b]
  Returns the synodic phase between two planets — their angular separation measured
in the direction of zodiacal motion (0-360°). This tracks where we are in the cycle
between consecutive conjunctions of two planets. 0° = conjunction (start of cycle),
90° = first quarter, 180° = opposition (midpoint of cycle), 270° = last quarter.
The Jupiter-Saturn synodic cycle (~20 years) is the backbone of long-term business
cycle analysis in financial astrology. The Sun-Moon synodic cycle is the familiar
lunar month (New Moon at 0°, Full Moon at 180°).
  Parameters:
    [b]p1 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) First planet (the faster one by convention, but either order works).
    [b]p2 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) Second planet (the slower one by convention).
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]preferGeo (bool)[/b]: (bool) If true, use geocentric longitudes. If false, use heliocentric.
  Returns: (float) Synodic phase angle in degrees, range [0, 360). 0° = conjunction, 180° = opposition.

[b]is_parallel(p1, p2, t, orb)[/b]
  Returns true when two planets are at the same declination (both the same distance
north or south of the celestial equator), within a given tolerance (orb). This is
called a "parallel" aspect and acts like a conjunction — the planets' energies merge.
Parallels operate independently of zodiac position, revealing hidden correlations
that longitude aspects miss entirely. Both W.D. Gann and George Bayer used parallels
as core trading indicators. The typical orb is 1°.
  Parameters:
    [b]p1 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) First planet.
    [b]p2 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) Second planet.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]orb (float)[/b]: (float) Tolerance in degrees (typically 1.0).
  Returns: (bool) True if both planets share the same declination within the given orb.

[b]is_contraparallel(p1, p2, t, orb)[/b]
  Returns true when two planets are at equal declination but on opposite sides of the
celestial equator (one north, one south), within a given tolerance (orb). This is
called a "contraparallel" and acts like an opposition — creating tension or polarity.
Like parallels, contraparallels are invisible in standard zodiacal charts and only
show up when you track declination. The typical orb is 1°.
  Parameters:
    [b]p1 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) First planet.
    [b]p2 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) Second planet.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]orb (float)[/b]: (float) Tolerance in degrees (typically 1.0).
  Returns: (bool) True if both planets are at equal declination on opposite sides of the equator.

[b]get_speed_diff(p1, p2, t)[/b]
  Returns the difference in geocentric speed between two planets (degrees/day).
George Bayer discovered that specific speed differentials predict market reversals.
His key rule: when the Mars-Mercury speed difference equals exactly 59 arcminutes
(about 0.983°/day), a major top or bottom forms within 3 days. He also tracked
individual planet speeds — Mercury at exactly 59 arcminutes or 1°58' per day
signals trend reversals.
  Parameters:
    [b]p1 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) First planet.
    [b]p2 (series Planet)[/b]: (Planet) Second planet.
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Speed difference in degrees/day: get_speed(p1) - get_speed(p2). Positive means p1 is moving faster.

[b]get_gmst(t)[/b]
  Returns Greenwich Mean Sidereal Time (GMST) — a measure of Earth's rotational
position relative to the stars. As Earth spins, different parts of the sky pass
overhead. GMST tells you which part of the celestial sphere is directly above
the Greenwich meridian (0° longitude) right now. This is the foundation for
calculating the Ascendant and Midheaven. One full sidereal day (360°) is about
23 hours 56 minutes — slightly shorter than a solar day because Earth also orbits
the Sun.
  Parameters:
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
  Returns: (float) Greenwich Mean Sidereal Time in degrees, range [0, 360).

[b]get_lst(t, observer_lon)[/b]
  Returns Local Sidereal Time (LST) — the sidereal time at your specific location
on Earth. This is simply GMST adjusted for your longitude. If you're east of
Greenwich, your local sidereal time is ahead; if west, it's behind. LST determines
which degree of the ecliptic is culminating (at the Midheaven) at your location.
  Parameters:
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]observer_lon (float)[/b]: (float) Observer's geographic longitude in degrees. East is positive, west is negative (e.g., New York = -74.0, London = 0.0, Tokyo = 139.7).
  Returns: (float) Local Sidereal Time in degrees, range [0, 360).

[b]get_angles(t, observer_lat, observer_lon)[/b]
  Returns the four angular house cusps: Ascendant (ASC), Descendant (DSC), Midheaven (MC),
and Imum Coeli (IC) — the four most powerful points in any astrological chart.

The Midheaven (MC) is the degree of the ecliptic that is highest in the sky at your
location right now — the point of culmination. It represents public life, career, and
visible achievements.

The Imum Coeli (IC) is directly opposite the MC (MC + 180°) — the lowest point below
the horizon. It represents roots, home, and private life.

The Ascendant (ASC) is the degree of the ecliptic rising on the eastern horizon at your
location right now. It changes by about 1° every 4 minutes, making it the most time-sensitive
point in a chart. It represents the self, first impressions, and beginnings.

The Descendant (DSC) is directly opposite the ASC (ASC + 180°) — the point setting on the
western horizon. It represents partnerships and "the other."

These four angles rotate through all 360° of the zodiac every ~24 hours as Earth spins.
They require your geographic latitude and longitude as inputs.
  Parameters:
    [b]t (float)[/b]: (float) Unix timestamp in milliseconds (use built-in 'time' variable).
    [b]observer_lat (float)[/b]: (float) Observer's geographic latitude in degrees. North is positive, south is negative (e.g., New York = 40.7, Sydney = -33.9).
    [b]observer_lon (float)[/b]: (float) Observer's geographic longitude in degrees. East is positive, west is negative (e.g., New York = -74.0, London = 0.0, Tokyo = 139.7).
  Returns: [ASC, DSC, MC, IC] Four ecliptic longitudes in degrees, each in range [0, 360).

[b]get_house_cusp_whole_sign(asc_lon, house_num)[/b]
  Returns the cusp (starting degree) of a house using the Whole Sign system.
This is the oldest house system, used in Hellenistic astrology. The entire sign
containing the Ascendant becomes the 1st house, the next sign becomes the 2nd house,
and so on. Each house spans exactly 30° (one full sign). Simple, unambiguous, and
increasingly popular in modern practice.
  Parameters:
    [b]asc_lon (float)[/b]: (float) Ascendant longitude in degrees (from get_angles).
    [b]house_num (int)[/b]: (int) House number, 1-12.
  Returns: (float) Ecliptic longitude of the house cusp in degrees, range [0, 360).

[b]get_house_cusp_equal(asc_lon, house_num)[/b]
  Returns the cusp (starting degree) of a house using the Equal House system.
Each house spans exactly 30° starting from the exact Ascendant degree (not the sign
boundary). For example, if the ASC is at 15° Taurus, the 1st house is 15° Taurus to
15° Gemini, the 2nd house is 15° Gemini to 15° Cancer, etc. This system is simple
and gives each house equal weight, but the MC may not align with the 10th house cusp.
  Parameters:
    [b]asc_lon (float)[/b]: (float) Ascendant longitude in degrees (from get_angles).
    [b]house_num (int)[/b]: (int) House number, 1-12.
  Returns: (float) Ecliptic longitude of the house cusp in degrees, range [0, 360).
